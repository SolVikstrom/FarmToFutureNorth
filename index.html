Statement

Mission and vision

Our mission is not to invent yet another food portal, an exclusive calendar, or a new app competing with what already exists. There are already dedicated pioneers, local initiatives, and micro-marketplaces out there. Our vision is instead to act as a catalyst and an orchestrator: setting a shared direction, building a strong narrative, and establishing a few clear anchor points—and then inviting the ecosystem to fill it with content.

A lever for the community champions. Our role is to create a shared lever. By establishing a compelling narrative and a handful of focal dates (such as four seasonal anchor days per year), we build a stage where other actors can easily attach their own initiatives under the same umbrella.

Low threshold for the giants – broader reach for the small. By keeping the concept extremely lean, we make it effortless for more rigid, large-scale actors (like municipal kitchens and retail grocery chains) to participate, while simultaneously giving regional food artisans and local enthusiasts a shared megaphone.

Understory

The everyday consumer

Fun theory and edutainment. Challenges everyday shoppers to break out of grocery autopilot through fun-theory and edutainment interventions. It transforms products into tangible storytellers of artisan craft—pairing a shared mark of authenticity with an invitation to explore the regional food landscape as a culinary traveler.

Understory

Public procurement and institutional innovation

Civic preparedness. Engages institutional buyers, meal planners, and municipal policymakers to pioneer flexible framework tenders, Dynamic Purchasing Systems (DPS), and regulatory adaptations—using civic preparedness and food security as primary catalysts.

Understory

From ego to ecosystem

Shifts the narrative from the isolated smallholder to a connected collective that speaks as a unified ‘WE’. By coordinating social campaigns, co-hosting creator takeovers, cross-promoting products, and sharing audiences, producers turn culinary craft into a shared sales magnet rather than a lonely marketing battle.

Problemstatement

The Growth Cap

Small and medium-sized food producers in the North are caught in a double structural squeeze that slows their growth:

The market and sales threshold. Producers lack the time, expertise, and reach to run digital sales funnels, manage AI and marketing tools, or engage larger buyers.

The logic of seasonality and volume. Harvests and raw material supply arrive in bursts dictated by weather and season, whereas the market expects a steady supply. Producers are both able and willing to grow and produce more, but they find too few buyers ready to take on large volumes at the exact moment of harvest. Without the capacity to easily handle, process, and store surpluses, a hard cap is placed on their potential production volumes and revenue.

Konceptutforskning

Local Food Catalyst

Connecting isolated smallholders into coordinated market power.

The concept explores how shared resources and functions can help local food producers build sustainable, profitable businesses. Designed in close collaboration with producers, stakeholders, and innovative services, this catalyst acts as a dynamic node for collective problem-solving and market reach.

The concept explores how a coordinating function might operate as a hybrid between an advocacy body and a producer management agency—representing smallholders as its “clients”.

The Sales and Narrative Orchestrator

The advocacy and campaign engine. On the market side, it curates and coordinates high-visibility formats, such as a shared annual harvest calendar and recurring theme days (inspired by Cinnamon Bun Day), making it effortless and attractive for grocery stores, food associations, and municipalities to plug in and spotlight regional food.

The producer management agency. On the producer side, it acts as a guide past digital thresholds, connecting producers to the right sales channels, and representing their interests toward wholesalers and public procurement.

The shared infrastructure and technological innovations orchestrator

Cold-chain and logistics consolidation. Exploring how shared collection points (like the Noah model) can link farms to existing distribution streams with one delivery and one invoice.

Shared preservation, storage and processing. Investigating how mobile or shared technical solutions (drying, canning, curing, cold storage) can turn raw crops into shelf-stable, value-added goods (like sauces and spice bases) across seasons.

Packaging and retail readiness. Defining shared support for GS1 barcodes, shelf-fit packaging, and provenance storytelling for retail and tourism channels.

04 · Affärsmodell

Exploring the business model and funding

A central part of our work is to investigate how such an orchestrator can become financially self-sustaining over time and generate long-term commercial value. Rather than locking into a single model, we will explore and evaluate a range of governance and revenue mechanisms. For example, it could be:

Producer co-ownership (economic association or limited company / AB). A jointly owned entity—such as a cooperative (ekonomisk förening) or a shared limited company (AB)—where participating producers and key partners hold shares, ensuring shared governance, risk-sharing, and reinvested returns.

Transaction-based fees (commission). A percentage-based fee on sales facilitated or consolidated through the node, keeping upfront entry barriers low for small-scale producers.

Pay-per-use service and equipment rental. Variable fees based on usage (e.g., per hour, weight, or pallet space) when accessing shared processing equipment, cold storage, or packaging support.

Public co-financing and preparedness mandates. Municipal or regional base funding for the coordinating function (“The Orchestrator”) and shared infrastructure, in exchange for strengthened regional food security and smoother public procurement.

Need to have eller nice to have. Vilka delar av konceptet är absolut mest kritiska för att producenter ska våga skala upp sin produktion (säljavlastning, samlastning eller förädlings-/lagringskapacitet för överskott) – och var lägger vi tyngdpunkten i vår utforskning?

Omvärldsbevakning

Vilka befintliga digitala verktyg, marknadsplatser och logistikmodeller (likt Noah, REKO eller delade maskinringar) finns redan på plats som orchestratorn kan bygga vidare på eller docka in i?

Möjliga huvudmän och iscensättning

Vilka befintliga aktörer i regionen skulle realistiskt kunna ta ägarskap och driva en sådan nod vidare (t.ex. ett producentkooperativ, en befintlig grossist/ankarföretag eller ett offentligt-privat samverkansbolag)?

03 · Store Owner

In my role as a Store Owner, I want to buy and display food, so that I can sell it with a good profit per metre of shelf.

I run a grocery store, choose the food we sell, and decide how to display it.

Threshold

Standardisation

As a retailer, all products on our shelves must comply with standardized barcodes, packaging footprints, and inventory routines. If local goods fall outside these operational baselines, handling them manually simply becomes too costly and complex for our staff.

How might we

help local micro-producers meet standard retail onboarding criteria, so their products integrate into store systems and shelves as smoothly as any conventional brand?

Threshold

Logistics integration

As a grocery store, our receiving docks rely on consolidated, scheduled deliveries—either via the central warehouse or approved distribution routes. Managing fragmented, individual deliveries from multiple small suppliers creates administrative and operational bottlenecks our store staff cannot absorb.

How might we

aggregate local micro-deliveries into existing retail distribution streams, making regional produce as frictionless to receive and process as warehouse shipments?

Threshold

Returns and terms

As a grocery store, I need predictable margins and protection against shelf waste, so I default to standard contracts with fixed prices and return clauses on unsold stock.

How might we

balance retail shelf-risk with producer cash flow, creating viable terms for small batches without punitive buy-back clauses?

Threshold

Turnover and margin

As an independent grocer, every shelf metre must earn its keep; slow-moving local products create shelf waste that our margins cannot defend.

How might we

drive steady shelf turnover for local products so they earn their place on high-traffic retail shelves?

What if

local makers shared a co-branded destination shelf that draws higher impulse traffic?

What if

producers delivered micro-batches on dynamic consignment, taking the waste risk off the grocer?

04 · Public meal provider

In my role as a public meal provider, I want to buy food, so that I can serve nutritious meals every day.

I cook for school kitchens, health care, and social care.

Threshold

Rules and procurement

As a municipality, public procurement rules and rigid framework agreements often steer us toward volume requirements and certifications that may inadvertently exclude individual smallholders.

How might we

adapt municipal tender lots and aggregation models so small-scale producers can qualify for public kitchens without creating legal or administrative risk for the municipality?

Threshold

Deliveries and invoices

As a municipal meals department, our kitchens rely on predictable, consolidated deliveries and single-invoice billing to keep meal schedules on track. Coordinating multiple small-scale producers with varying delivery times, broken cold chains, and fragmented paperwork adds operational complexity our kitchens and administration cannot absorb.

How might we

make sourcing from small local producers as operationally and administratively frictionless for municipal kitchens as buying from a single distributor?

What if

an intermediary actor (like the Noah model) acted as a shared logistics and sales hub, aggregating orders and deliveries into a single invoice for public kitchens?

What if

an automated digital intake platform synchronized local producers’ availability directly with the municipality’s existing ordering system, turning separate small orders into one consolidated batch?

What if

the municipality’s existing framework distributor was contracted to provide cross-docking services, carrying local smallholders’ crates on their regular morning school delivery routes?

Threshold

Cost per portion

As a public meal provider, our operations are bound to strict, fixed cost-per-portion limits across thousands of daily school and eldercare meals. While we value regional quality, we cannot absorb higher unit prices from small producers without exceeding statutory budget caps.

How might we

introduce local ingredients into public meal planning—such as through menu redesign, seasonal menu shifts, or partial blending—so producers receive fair compensation without breaching municipal portion budgets?

07 · Primary and artisan producer

In my role as a primary and/or artisan producer, I want to expose my harvest to potential buyers, at the right time, in the right place, with the right story, so that it is easy for them to buy it.

I grow and harvest food and sell it to shops, restaurants, and local customers.

Threshold

Season and volume

As a producer, my harvest and volume are governed by the weather and the season, but customers are used to the same range all year, and contracts want steadiness.

How might we

turn natural seasonal variations into a unique value proposition?

What if

we established a few high-impact national food days with local focus — like Cinnamon Bun Day—as catalysts that draw consumers into a year-round seasonal calendar, aligning in-store promotions, harvest happenings, related food destinations and regional recipes?

Threshold

Price and effort

As a producer, it is difficult to capture fair financial value for the labour and resources invested, while simultaneously navigating and managing an optimal mix of sales channels.

How might we

help primary producers capture premium returns with minimal operational drag?

What if

we provided a unified ecosystem map that guides producers to the right digital sales channels and micro-marketplaces (like FreshNearby, Graani, or Picksy)—matching their specific volume, shelf-life, and customer segments with zero guesswork?

What if

producers could manage their inventory and product stories in one single entry point that automatically feeds into existing regional funnels, bots, and local shops—removing the operational drag of juggling multiple platforms?

Threshold

Transport and distribution

As a remote food producer, delivering to dispersed, distant customers is financially unviable unless orders reach critical volume thresholds, forcing me to either swallow disproportionate delivery costs or lose sales to inaccessible buyers.

How might we

nudge and enable customers to coordinate and share the delivery costs of purchasing from remote artisan producers, turning isolated single orders into viable collective drop-offs?

What if

customers could share a live order link with neighbors or colleagues, unlocking free or subsidized home/office delivery once a collective neighborhood basket hits a threshold?

What if

pre-order drops operated with dynamic group discounts or shared flat freight, where each additional neighbor who joins directly lowers the shipping cost for everyone?

What if

passionate food lovers were nudged and incentivized to become local “neighborhood hosts” or pick-up points in exchange for store credits or free produce?

What if

digital checkouts featured a “delivery buddy” or geo-matching prompt showing: “3 households in your postal code are ordering this week—add your items before Thursday to share the run”?
